The line — ten waters, one engine

Ten fishing watch games off one engine, each water paired with its classic lure. Cast from the wrist, soak, work the lure with taps; the fish strikes and leaps trying to spit the hook while your finger holds tension on the line. Land it and the catch mounts on the trophy wall as a clock — new size, new colors, every catch: young, prime, old growth, or a dinosaur. In the operator's store the faces ride the house's round-dollar shelf — $10 a face — and the whole line lands inside the operator package below.

The board & the Triangle

The board is free. Anybody who lands a fish posts to the leaderboard — species records, placings against dated house records, days-unbroken counters that reset in public when a record falls, and the monster board for the dinosaurs. No charge, ever: the board is the spine of the arcade, and it stays open so the water stays full. Catches post to the board from every face automatically.

The Triangle is the pinnacle. Place three fish of one species on the board — first, second, third, any mix — and you unlock the Arrangement Mount: your three fish as talking heads on your wrist and your dashboard, telling the time and the date. It is the top trophy in the house, it is earned on the water, and it cannot be bought. Money speeds nothing at the top.

The community stance — YouTube first

The arcade is pre-built for a community that watches together. The stance is YouTube-first, because that is where the community already sits: the channel is the arcade's front porch, and the slots below are cut into the package from the start — not bolted on later.

Tournament broadcasts. The rounds, called live — the community's own fishing competitions, fished virtually: every player at his own desk, on his own monitor, on the same clock, with the board keeping score in the open.

The daily drop. A short film in the Shot-of-the-Day pattern — one catch, one story, every day the water is open. The community sees its own fish on the screen, not stock footage of somebody else's lake.

Community catches. The community films itself — the fight, the landing, the mount on the wall. Somebody will film it straight, somebody will film it in a bass costume, somebody will LARP the whole tournament. The arcade does not care what it becomes: the journalism intake below takes the evidence, and the record keeps it straight.

Seat one was taken by a sportsman whose whole scope, in his own words, was "this is what my community wants" — a personal, local interest in his own people. That is the customer this stance is built for: the operator runs his community; the arcade gives the community somewhere to stand.

Tournament journalism intake

Fishing a community round? File the catch here — this is the journalism desk for the virtual competitions. Every filed catch carries the entrant, the species, the fish's numbers, the evidence, and the time it was caught, and lands on the house desk to be checked against the board. A record broken inside a tournament still posts to the board, with its days-unbroken counter reset in public: the board never lies for a round.

ROUNDS STAGED The intake desk is built and standing; tournament rounds open when the first round is announced on the community's channel. How the money works, in the open: buy-ins ride store credits and in-app entry — the clean lanes. Prizes pay in store credits and build credits — house credit that spends inside the house and never converts to cash. Shares for first, second, and third, and the house's cut, print on the round card before a round opens — the cut's rate is set in person and stays off this page until it is. A cash payout lane exists on the drawing board only: it does not run, and no copy here offers it.
